# Insurance, contracts, and the "can I wait?" question

## Short answer

You can get quotes now, and you can bind a policy the same week a client says yes. **Do not start billable work before the policy is active.** The gap between "they agreed" and "I am covered" is exactly where the expensive mistakes live.

You do not need insurance to have a website, send proposals, or take a free call. You do need it before you touch a client's accounts, data, or network — especially if the client asks for a certificate of insurance (COI) as part of onboarding.

## What to buy

A solo IT consultant usually needs a small package, not five separate policies.

1. **Technology professional liability (tech E&O)** — covers claims that your work caused a financial loss: a failed migration, a missed backup, a configuration that took down email. This is the core policy.
2. **Cyber liability** — covers a breach or ransomware event touching data you hold or systems you manage: incident response, notification costs, regulatory defense, sometimes data restoration. Check whether it covers your acts and your subcontracted tools, not just your own network.
3. **General liability** — covers bodily injury or property damage while you are on a client site. Often bundled; may be required by a landlord or a client's vendor policy. A BOP or a technology package can include it.
4. **Workers' compensation** — only if you have employees. A sole proprietor with no employees generally does not need it, but check the rules for any state where you work and any client contract that requires it.

If you form an LLC, the entity should be the named insured, and you should be listed as an individual where the policy allows.

## What it costs

For a solo consultant with no employees and modest revenue, expect roughly:

- Tech E&O + cyber bundle: **$500–$1,500 per year** for $1M per claim / $1M–$2M aggregate, depending on revenue, services, and deductible.
- General liability, if not bundled: **$300–$800 per year**.
- Higher limits or on-site network work can push the total above that.

Monthly installments are common. A policy is usually **claims-made**, which means the coverage responds to claims reported while the policy is active (and any extended reporting period you buy). The retroactive date matters: work you did before the policy's retro date may not be covered. Ask for a retro date that goes back to your first engagement, or buy prior-acts coverage.

## Where to get quotes

Ask at least three. These are common for small technology consultancies in California:

- **Hiscox** — small-business technology and cyber packages; online quotes.
- **Next Insurance** — small-business professional liability, often fast online.
- **Thimble** — short-term and annual professional liability; useful for a single project.
- **TechInsurance / Insureon** — brokers that specialize in technology risks; they will compare carriers.
- **Simply Business** — small-business brokerage with technology options.
- **Embroker** or **Vouch** — startup-oriented, usually better once revenue is higher.
- A **local independent broker** who writes technology risks. Ask what the policy excludes: data restoration, regulatory defense, breach notification, and subcontractor acts are the usual gaps.

Ask each one:

- Is this claims-made or occurrence? What is the retroactive date?
- Does cyber cover ransomware, business email compromise, and breach notification?
- Does the E&O cover mistakes by a subcontractor or a tool I recommend?
- Is on-site network work excluded?
- Can I add a client as a certificate holder without changing the policy?
- What is the deductible, and is defense outside the limit?

## Timing, step by step

1. **Now (free):** get three quotes. Save the quotes as PDFs. You do not pay until you bind.
2. **When a client says yes:** confirm the scope, then bind the policy before the kickoff. Many carriers can bind online the same day; a broker may need 24–48 hours.
3. **Before you start:** get the COI with the client named as certificate holder if they require it. Check the dates and limits match the contract.
4. **Every renewal:** review revenue and services. If you start doing network installations or handling health data, tell the carrier. A policy that does not know what you do is a policy that can deny a claim.

## Can you wait until a client wants to pay?

Yes — with two conditions:

- **Get the quotes now.** Waiting until the client is ready and then discovering that underwriting takes a week is how you lose the job.
- **Do not perform the work before coverage is bound.** You can sign the contract, invoice the deposit, and schedule the kickoff. You should not log into their tenant, touch their backups, or change their network until the policy is active.

If a client requires a COI before they will sign, you need to bind first. That is normal and is a good reason to have the quote ready.

## The contract: MSA + SOW

You need two documents, not one.

- **Master Services Agreement (MSA)** — the standing terms: confidentiality, data handling, IP ownership, limitation of liability, indemnity, payment terms, termination, governing law.
- **Statement of Work (SOW)** — the specific engagement: scope, deliverables, exclusions, timeline, fees, client responsibilities, acceptance criteria.

Minimum clauses for IT work:

- **Scope and exclusions** — what you will and will not do. Say explicitly that you are not providing legal advice, compliance certification, or 24/7 monitoring unless separately agreed.
- **Client responsibilities** — access, backups, approvals, and the person authorized to approve changes.
- **Change control** — scope changes are quoted and approved in writing before work continues.
- **Fees and payment** — deposit, milestones, net terms, late fee, suspension for non-payment.
- **Limitation of liability** — cap at fees paid (or a multiple), exclude consequential damages. Your insurer will care about this.
- **Confidentiality and data handling** — NDA terms, least privilege, deletion after handover, breach notification.
- **IP and deliverables** — client owns the deliverables and accounts on full payment; you retain pre-existing tools and general know-how.
- **Warranty and acceptance** — a short warranty period, a defined acceptance process, no guarantee of a specific business outcome.
- **Termination** — notice period, payment for work performed, transition assistance.
- **Independent contractor** — no employment relationship, you control how the work is done.
- **Governing law** — California, and a venue in Riverside County if you are based in the Coachella Valley.

Where to get it:

- A technology lawyer can review a template for roughly **$500–$1,500**; a full custom MSA can be more. This is worth it before your first $5,000 engagement.
- Reputable template services (Hello Bonsai, DocuSign templates, LawDepot) are a starting point, not a substitute for review. Do not copy a template from a random site.
- Keep a signed PDF of every SOW, and do not start work on a verbal yes.

## California housekeeping

- **Entity:** a sole proprietorship is the default and is fine to start, but it does not separate your personal assets. A California LLC has an **$800 minimum franchise tax** per year, so do the math with a CPA. Insurance is not a substitute for an entity, and an entity is not a substitute for insurance.
- **Business license:** check the city where you are based (Palm Desert, Palm Springs, Indio, etc.) for a business tax certificate or license.
- **Seller's permit:** generally not required for consulting services, but check with the CDTFA if you sell tangible goods.
- **Taxes:** set aside for self-employment tax; make quarterly estimated payments. A separate business bank account from day one makes this much easier.
- **Privacy:** if you handle health information, you may be a business associate under HIPAA. Do not take that on without a BAA, the right insurance, and a clear scope. The same caution applies to payment card data and financial records.

## What to put on the website, and when

Do not write "insured" until the policy is bound. Once it is:

- Add an FAQ answer: "Do you carry insurance?" — "Yes. I carry technology professional liability and cyber liability coverage, and I can provide a certificate of insurance for engagements that require one."
- Add a line to the SOW/proposal template.
- Keep the COI request path simple: client legal name, address, and the required limits.

Until then, the site stays silent on insurance. That is better than a claim you cannot back up.
